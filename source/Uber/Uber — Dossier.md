## 1. Business anatomy — Acquire / Engage / Retain / Monetize

- **Acquire** — Barbell entry products pull in new segments: Moto/Wait & Save (cheap, +75% frequency, later upgrade to UberX), plus Women Preferred, Teens, and a simple older-demographic product [S1][S2]; each new product or use-case adds a +25% first-time-acquisition lift [S1]. Cross-app is the cheapest channel — 30% of eligible Mobility users haven't tried Eats, and ~$15B a year already flows app-to-app [S1]. In AV cities, novelty and lower price pull first-time riders faster than the rest of the country [S2]. New countries add fresh audience (7 new Delivery markets; Finland hit #1 on the App Store at launch) [S1]. An "AI-enabled" sales force speeds up merchant sign-ups [S2].
- **Engage** — The core loop is liquidity: more riders → shorter ETAs and lower prices → more drivers. Frequency comes from the low-end products (+75% frequency) and destination prediction (AI pre-predicts 3/4 of rides) [S1]. The aha moment differs by mode — a reliable 3-minute ETA on Mobility; grocery/retail selection on Delivery. Multi-product deepens the relationship: 40% use more than one product, and each added product raises retention [S2].
- **Retain** — Uber One is the retention spine: >50M members, >50% of bookings, "very, very sticky" across food, grocery and AV [S1][S2]. Switching costs come from accumulated perks (no delivery fees, no fees above a $60 grocery basket, 10% hotel credits that pay for the membership) [S1]. Steady prices (enabled by the insurance flip) protect long-run demand and cut churn from price shocks [S2]. Management names "strong" retention as a Delivery growth driver [S1].
- **Monetize** — Four lines: Mobility (take-rate on rides, +20% GB, record margins) [S1]; Delivery (take-rate plus grocery/retail, +23% GB) [S1]; Freight (logistics, low margin, back to growth) [S1]; Advertising (>2% of delivery GBs, room to grow from enterprise plus early grocery/retail/mobility ad products) [S2]. Uber One membership fees are a fifth line and the anchor for the other four. Price fences: barbell tiers (Wait & Save vs Reserve/premium), member-only fee waivers, and Reserve's higher margin [S1][S2].

## 3. Wow Vault ★
*The selective, non-obvious layer. Ranked strongest first.*

**★ Uber added 50× the trips the entire AV industry added — the "AV disruption" is a rounding error against Uber's base**
- **Picture:** In one year Uber added 50× the trips the whole autonomous-vehicle (AV, self-driving) industry added combined [S2]. Uber runs about 15B trips a year [S2]; AV players add a sliver.
- **Why non-obvious:** The market prices Uber as the victim of AVs. The scale gap flips the story: for years, AVs are extra supply Uber routes, not a rival network.
- **Deploy:** "Is Waymo an existential threat to Uber?" — recall hook: *"50× the trips."*
- **Source:** [S2]

**★ AVs are net-positive for ridesharing — they grow the pie, not eat it**
- **Mechanism:** In SF (where Waymo competes), Austin and Atlanta (AVs on Uber), gross bookings sped up, new-rider growth beat the rest of the country, and trip frequency held [S2]. Q1'26: "we don't see any effect of the Waymo launches on our overall business"; Uber's category position in SF and L.A. is higher than six months earlier [S1].
- **Why non-obvious:** You'd expect a rival robotaxi to steal trips. The data shows AV availability grows the market (lower price, novelty pull new riders) faster than it takes trips away.
- **Deploy:** cannibalization / competitive-response questions — recall hook: *"expansionary, not substitutive."*
- **Source:** [S1][S2]

**★ An AV on Uber's network does 30% more trips/day than the same AV run alone — Uber is the best home for a robot**
- **Mechanism:** AVs on Uber do "30% higher" trips per vehicle per day than a standalone first-party (1P) fleet, with better ETAs; "the best product today out there in the market is an AV on the Uber platform" [S2]. Demand pools across rides, eats and freight, so the vehicle sits idle less.
- **Why non-obvious:** Uber's role changes from "middleman the AV players will skip" to "the tool AV players need to keep vehicles busy" — the case for a robotaxi maker to list on Uber instead of going direct.
- **Deploy:** "Won't Waymo/Tesla just go direct?" — recall hook: *"30% higher utilization."*
- **Source:** [S2]

**★ Trough utilization is a structural moat only Uber has**
- **Mechanism:** Rideshare demand drops about 45% from the Saturday peak to Monday; an AV fleet paid for on 24/7 economics loses money in that trough. Uber can fill idle AVs with Delivery and Freight loads — "a structural advantage not available to any other player" [S2]. This is why Freight and Delivery matter to the AV case even at low margin.
- **Why non-obvious:** Explains why Uber insists on keeping low-margin Freight: it's the shock absorber that makes AV fleet financing bankable.
- **Deploy:** "Why does Uber keep Freight?" / AV unit-economics — recall hook: *"fill the Saturday-to-Monday trough."*
- **Source:** [S2]

**★ Insurance flipped from Uber's worst cost problem into a pricing weapon**
- **Mechanism:** After COVID, rising insurance cost was passed to riders as price hikes [S2]. Reform plus "hundreds of millions of dollars of savings" turned insurance into a cost that now falls with scale, for the first time since COVID [S1][S2]. Uber hands the savings back as lower or flat prices, and riders respond: "really good elasticity… price reduction translates to acceleration in trip growth" [S1]. L.A., the worst insurance market, is now "significantly better than California and the rest of the country" [S1].
- **Why non-obvious:** US Mobility's speed-up looks like demand magic; it's a cost-line mechanic. "Price consistency has a huge impact on long-term elasticity" [S2].
- **Deploy:** "What's driving US Mobility acceleration?" — recall hook: *"insurance went from headwind to leverage."*
- **Source:** [S1][S2]

**★ ~75% of US profit comes from the 70% of the US *outside* the top cities — and AVs won't reach it for years**
- **Picture:** 30% of bookings come from major cities; 70% of the US is outside top markets and "nearly 75% of our US profits come from those markets" [S2]. Those thin markets grow 1.5–2× faster than dense cities [S1][S2].
- **Why non-obvious:** The AV threat sits in a few dense downtowns. Uber's profit sits exactly where AVs are "unlikely to address… for a long time" — a durable non-AV moat in plain sight.
- **Deploy:** AV-threat and profit-durability questions — recall hook: *"the profit is in the suburbs."*
- **Source:** [S1][S2]

**★ AI removes the fixed-menu constraint — the interface becomes per-user**
- **Reframe:** A fixed UI is "the optimized average of your users… some users may not see what you've got to offer. AI solves all that… the UI is whatever the user wants that UI to be" [S1]. Uber already predicts your destination with AI on 3 of every 4 rides [S1].
- **Why non-obvious:** Positions AI at Uber as demand prediction and per-user personalization, not a bolted-on chatbot — turning a fixed-menu marketplace into one that adapts to each rider.
- **Deploy:** "How does AI change Uber's product?" — recall hook: *"the UI is whatever the user wants it to be."*
- **Source:** [S1]

**★ The hotels perk pays for the membership — a self-funding retention loop**
- **Mechanism:** Uber Reserve showed riders will pre-book, not just ride on demand [S1], lowering the risk of a 700,000-hotel expansion via Expedia; the economics come back to members as 10% Uber One credits. "A long weekend in New York City, that's getting $100 back, which pays for your entire Uber One membership for the year" [S1].
- **Why non-obvious:** One high-ticket category (hotels) can fund a full year of membership, so Uber One's retention pays for itself rather than dragging on margin.
- **Deploy:** membership / retention economics — recall hook: *"one hotel weekend pays for the year."*
- **Source:** [S1]

**★ Uber One is now >50% of bookings and grew +20M members in a single year**
- **Picture:** More than 50M members, up 50% year over year, and "more than 50% of our bookings" [S1]; ended 2024 at 30M, so +20M in one year [S1]. Members are "very, very sticky" across food, grocery, chargers and AV rides [S2].
- **Why non-obvious:** Most of Uber's booking volume now runs through a subscription. That's a higher-retention business than the pay-per-trip marketplace investors remember.
- **Deploy:** retention / LTV / defensibility — recall hook: *"half of bookings are members now."*
- **Source:** [S1][S2]

**★ 10% of Uber's code is now written by AI agents — and it blew through the AI budget**
- **Picture:** "About 10% of our code now is… built by agents"; AI cost is "offset by slower headcount growth" [S1]. Management says it "underestimated the amount of impact the AI tools could have" when budgeting in Nov, and has already "re-upped" past the original full-year AI budget [S1].
- **Why non-obvious:** A rare, honest admission that AI spend is running ahead of plan — and a concrete internal-productivity number, not a slogan.
- **Deploy:** AI-cost / operating-leverage questions — recall hook: *"blew the AI budget by Q1."*
- **Source:** [S1]

**★ "We've seen this movie before" — the metasearch analogy against agent disintermediation**
- **Reframe:** On personal AI agents (Meta/Google/OpenAI) hiding Uber behind them, Dara points to travel metasearch: "most of the value of those front ends accrued to the large… consolidated players — Expedia, Airbnb, Booking.com" [S1]. Uber will open APIs to Apple/OpenAI/Claude/Gemini but "the majority of our transactions come direct" [S1].
- **Why non-obvious:** Takes the scariest bear case (Uber becomes a dumb supplier behind an AI agent) and shows the supply-side consolidator, not the front-end, captured the value last time.
- **Deploy:** "Do AI agents commoditize Uber?" — recall hook: *"we've seen this movie before — metasearch."*
- **Source:** [S1]

## 4. Reframes & mental models to borrow
*The company's own framing devices, restated to use on any prompt.*

- **"Winner-take-most in 3P."** Uber grants that AV hardware and software will have many players (Waymo, Pony, WeRide, Baidu, Waabi, Wayve, Nuro, Avride, NVIDIA-standardized) [S2]; it claims to win most of the third-party demand layer they all route through. → competitive-moat, platform-strategy questions.
- **"AVs are expansionary."** New robotaxi supply lowers price and adds novelty, pulling new riders faster than it takes existing trips [S1][S2]. → cannibalization / market-sizing.
- **"Barbell portfolio."** Grow the low end (Wait & Save, Moto, +75% higher frequency) and the high end (Reserve, XXL/premium, 3.5× higher profit growth) at the same time; both add a 25% first-time-acquisition lift [S1][S2]. → product-portfolio / prioritization.
- **"The UI is whatever the user wants it to be."** AI removes the fixed-menu limit of a marketplace [S1]. → AI-product design.
- **"Fill the trough."** A marketplace is worth as much as it can monetize idle supply in slow hours; Delivery and Freight give the AV fleet baseline demand [S2]. → unit-economics / operations.
- **"Marriott doesn't own its hotels."** AV fleets get financed by PE firms and banks (like data centers), so Uber scales AV supply without owning it [S2]. → capital-allocation / balance-sheet.
- **"Democratize AV data."** Uber and NVIDIA pool 3M+ hours of real-world pickup/drop-off data and give it to the whole ecosystem, erasing the small AV player's biggest disadvantage [S2] and stopping any single AV maker from winning. → platform / coopetition strategy.
- **"On-demand → planned."** Reserve proved users will pre-book, opening airports and hotels as new planned-demand surfaces [S1]. → new-market entry / adjacency.

## 6. AI impact — deflationary or moat?

| AI vector | Deflationary / commoditizing | Moat-deepening / compounding |
|---|---|---|
| Autonomous vehicles | AV supply could replace drivers and let makers go direct (1P) [S1][S2] | But +30% utilization on Uber, trough backfill, and 3P demand aggregation make Uber the best home for a robot — it grows the market, not shrinks it [S2] |
| Personal AI agents (front-end) | Meta/Google/OpenAI agents could reduce Uber to a dumb supplier [S1] | Metasearch precedent: value went to supply consolidators; most transactions stay direct; Uber opens APIs on its own terms [S1] |
| AI-driven personalization / UI | Generic AI UX is universal and easy to copy | "UI is whatever the user wants" plus destination prediction on 3/4 of rides runs on Uber's own demand data — rivals lack it [S1] |
| Coding/ops automation | Available to every company | 10% agent-written code, AI-enabled sales force, headcount offset — compounds on Uber's scale [S1][S2] |
| AV-data factory (NVIDIA) | Sharing data lowers the moat for small AV makers | …which is the point — it stops any single AV maker from winning, keeping supply fragmented and 3P-dependent [S2] |

**Net read:** Net **tailwind**. AI's two scariest vectors (AVs, agent front-ends) both turn into demand-aggregation strengths, and Uber's own asset — global real-time demand plus a suburban profit pool AVs can't reach — is exactly what AI can't copy. **The one real AI risk to watch:** a single dominant AV maker (Tesla) getting enough owned supply and demand in dense metros to go direct and skip the 3P layer, breaking the "winner-take-most in 3P" thesis in Uber's most visible markets.

## 7. Needs-based segments → problems → solutions
*Shreyas method: segment on NEEDS, not demographics. Each passes the 5-Point Test.*

**Segmentation basis:** Cut on the two needs that govern marketplace behavior — **(1) trip predictability need** (spontaneous/on-demand ↔ planned/scheduled) and **(2) price-vs-certainty need** (cost-minimizing ↔ certainty/experience-maximizing). Demographics (teens, women, older) are just access wrappers around these needs, not segments themselves.

**A. The cost-minimizing spontaneous rider** — Job: get there now for as little as possible (functional); feel smart about the deal (personal). Friction: surge/price shocks kill trust; may fall back to transit or walking. Nudge (intrinsic): predictable low price. Aha: *"Wait & Save saved me $4 and still came in 8 minutes."* Today: Wait & Save, Moto, +75% frequency [S1][S2]. Gap: still price-volatile in surge; sparse markets under-supplied. → **Play #1 (suburban liquidity), Play #4 (price stability from insurance savings).**

**B. The certainty-maximizing planner** — Job: make sure I catch the flight or meeting (functional); no anxiety (emotional). Friction: on-demand can't promise a car at 5am. Nudge (extrinsic): guaranteed pickup + higher driver pay. Aha: *"Reserve had a car waiting the second I landed."* Today: Reserve (growing well ahead of mainline, higher margin), airport-on-landing pickup, airports ~15% of Mobility GB [S1]. Gap: planned surfaces beyond airports are underbuilt. → **Play #2 (planned-life bundle: hotels + events).**

**C. The experience/status-maximizing rider** — Job: arrive comfortable and impress (social/personal). Friction: premium feels like an afterthought next to black-car services. Nudge: 3.5× profit-growth premium tier. Aha: *"XXL for the airport run with the whole family."* Today: premium/XXL/shuttle barbell, 3.5× profit growth [S1][S2]. Gap: premium under-cross-sold to members. → **Play #5 (premium × membership).**

**D. The multi-service household** — Job: run daily life (rides + groceries + food) from one place (functional); one bill, one loyalty (personal). Friction: 30% of Mobility users have never tried Eats; separate mental models. Nudge (intrinsic): Uber One perks stack across services. Aha: *"My hotel credit paid for the membership that waives my grocery fees."* Today: 40% multi-product, $15B cross-app Delivery GB, >50% of bookings from members [S1][S2]. Gap: 30% Mobility→Eats untapped; grocery still early. → **Play #3 (cross-service concierge), Play #6 (grocery selection).**

**E. The sparse-market / suburban user** — Job: get service that "just works" where density is thin (functional); not feel like a second-class market. Friction: historically longer ETAs, worse Delivery selection outside cities. Nudge (extrinsic): dedicated supply + selection. Aha: *"Uber works out here now, and faster than it used to."* Today: sparse markets grow 1.5–2× faster, ~75% of US profit, only ~20% of trips [S1][S2]. Gap: still under-penetrated; the profit engine with the most headroom. → **Play #1 (suburban liquidity).**

## 8. Strategy *(Shreyas Strategy Template)*
- **One-sentence strategy:** Own the third-party demand layer for all local movement — rides, food, freight — so that whoever supplies the vehicle (human driver today, AV tomorrow) routes through Uber's liquidity, membership, and utilization advantage.
- **Prioritize / Don't over-serve:** Prioritize suburban/sparse-market liquidity, Uber One depth, and AV 3P partnerships. Don't over-serve dense-downtown vanity metrics or a race to own AV hardware.
- **Pillars (moat → segment):** (1) Liquidity scale → cost-minimizers; (2) Membership + cross-sell → multi-service households; (3) Planned-demand products (Reserve/hotels) → planners; (4) Suburban profit pool → sparse-market users; (5) AV utilization + trough backfill → the AV supply side.
- **North star:** Gross bookings per active member per period (captures liquidity × frequency × cross-sell × retention in one number).
- **Non-priorities (trade-offs):** Not owning AV fleets at scale (finance them instead) [S2]; not chasing front-end AI agents (open APIs, stay the supply consolidator) [S1]; accepting lower Delivery margin to win grocery/retail share [S1].
- **Roadmap / metrics:** **Now** — US Mobility acceleration via insurance-funded price stability (leading: price-consistency index; lagging: US Mobility GB growth) [S1]. **Next** — AV to 15 cities, >30 partners (leading: AV trips Y/Y, now >10×; lagging: AV share of Mobility GB) [S1]. **Later** — planned-life bundle (hotels/events) + advertising scale-up (leading: Reserve/hotels attach; lagging: ad % of delivery GB past 2%) [S1][S2].

## 9. Contrarian bets & open tensions
- **Bet: AVs help Uber more than they hurt it.** Bear: a robotaxi is a direct substitute; makers with capital go direct and skip the 3P tax. Counter: +30% utilization, trough backfill, 50× scale, and category position rising in Waymo cities [S1][S2]. **Best skeptic angle:** Tesla is the one player with owned supply, demand and capital to go direct in dense metros — the exact markets with the highest visibility.
- **Bet: keep low-margin Freight and Delivery.** Bear: dilutive to margins, a distraction. Counter: they fill the trough that makes AV fleet financing bankable — strategic, not standalone [S2].
- **Bet: finance AV fleets, don't own them.** Bear: relies on PE and banks funding depreciating hardware with no resale liquidity. Counter: predictable revenue per vehicle per day, at a premium to what 1P makers get, makes the cash flows financeable, "just like Marriott doesn't own its hotels" [S1][S2].
- **Bet: stay the supply consolidator, give up the AI front-end.** Bear: personal agents own the customer and the margin. Counter: metasearch history says value pools with the supply consolidator; most transactions come direct [S1].
- **Valuation tension:** FWD P/E 24.56 on a $151.51B cap [S2] prices in durable 20%+ GB growth and the AV upside — yet Q1'26 revenue grew only 14.48% and missed [S1]. If the rev/GB gap (take-rate or mix) is structural rather than timing, the multiple is exposed. That gap is the single best short thesis.

## 10. Big Picture — Looks Wrong, Is Right

**A. Wise refusals** — what critics say Uber should do, but not doing it is the right call.
- **Don't build/own an AV stack to compete with Waymo/Tesla** → the restraint is correct: AV hardware is splitting into many suppliers with NVIDIA setting a standard [S2]; the durable rent is the 3P demand layer they all need for +30% utilization, not the commoditizing robot. Owning a stack would cost neutrality (why would a rival maker list on a competitor?). [S1][S2]
- **Don't build a consumer AI agent to fight OpenAI/Google front-ends** → correct: metasearch showed the front-end doesn't capture the value; Uber stays the supply consolidator and opens APIs on its own terms [S1].
- **Don't chase dense-downtown share against AVs** → correct: ~75% of US profit is in the suburbs AVs can't reach for years [S2]; fighting for low-profit downtown trips is a vanity war.
- **Don't own AV fleets on the balance sheet** → correct: predictable per-vehicle economics let PE and banks finance them capital-light; owning depreciating hardware with no resale liquidity is the trap [S1][S2].

**B. Counterintuitive moves** — what Uber does that looks like a mistake but serves a bigger play.
- **Keeping low-margin Freight and pushing low-margin grocery/retail** → the bigger play: Freight and Delivery fill the ~45% weekend demand trough, making AV fleets financeable — a moat no pure-play has [S2].
- **Handing AV data to the whole ecosystem via NVIDIA (3M+ hours)** → the bigger play: sharing data stops any single AV maker from building a data moat, keeping supply fragmented and 3P-dependent [S2].
- **Returning insurance savings to riders as lower prices instead of banking the margin** → the bigger play: because demand responds strongly, price cuts speed up trip growth and cement steady prices, compounding long-run demand — worth more than the near-term margin [S1][S2].
- **Investing past the AI budget mid-year** → the bigger play: agent-written code and an AI sales force offset headcount; getting the productivity curve early beats hitting a budget number [S1].
- **Committing capital to buy the first ~25,000 Waabi AVs (exclusive)** → the bigger play: seed exclusive supply to bootstrap AV liquidity, then hand off to financing — a temporary capital step to win the offtake, not a permanent ownership model [S2].

## 13. Interview arsenal

**A. The Deconstruct Set**

**1. Why this company?**
The market prices Uber as the AV wave's victim; the dossier's own numbers say the opposite. In one year Uber added 50 times the trips the entire autonomous-vehicle industry added combined, and an AV running on Uber's network does 30% more trips per vehicle per day than the same AV run standalone (§3 Wow Vault). The one-sentence strategy is to own the third-party demand layer for all local movement — rides, food, freight — so that whoever supplies the vehicle, human driver today or AV tomorrow, routes through Uber's liquidity, membership, and utilization advantage (§8 Strategy). That reduces to one clean, checkable bet: does Uber stay the neutral aggregator every AV maker needs, or does a well-capitalized rival eventually go direct?

**2. What do you like about it?**
The mechanic tying Freight and Delivery to the AV bet: rideshare demand drops about 45% from the Saturday peak to the Monday trough, and Uber fills idle AV capacity with delivery and freight loads — a structural advantage no AV-only or rideshare-only rival can copy (§3 Wow Vault, §5 Moats). Pair that with the suburban profit pool — roughly 75% of US profit comes from the 70% of the US outside major cities, growing 1.5–2× faster, and AVs are "unlikely to address… for a long time" (§3, §5) — and Uber's moat sits exactly where the disruption story doesn't reach. Handing 3M+ hours of AV pickup/dropoff data to the whole ecosystem via NVIDIA is the counterintuitive one: it stops any single AV maker from building its own data moat, keeping AV supply fragmented and dependent on Uber's 3P layer (§3, §4 Reframes: "Democratize AV data").

**3. What don't you like about it?**
The revenue-to-bookings gap is the sharpest crack: Q1'26 revenue grew 14.48% while gross bookings grew 21%, a spread pointing to take-rate or mix pressure, alongside a ~$60M miss even as EPS beat (§5 Weaknesses, §9 Contrarian bets). Delivery's margin held up only because mix hasn't fully shifted toward lower-margin grocery/retail yet — fragile if that trend continues (§5). And the AV thesis is a bet on other companies' choices: it depends on AV makers choosing to list on Uber's 3P layer instead of building direct, and Tesla is named as the one player with the capital, owned supply, and demand to skip that layer in dense metros (§5, §9). On top of that, management admits it under-budgeted AI spend and had to re-up mid-year (§5, §11), Advertising sits stuck below a 2% penetration ceiling management itself now calls too low (§11), and 30% of eligible Mobility riders have never tried Eats despite $15B a year already flowing app-to-app (§11) — self-admitted execution gaps, not bear-case speculation.

**4. How would you fix that?**
Close the revenue/GB gap with the highest-margin lever already on the balance sheet: Advertising, which management flags as under-ambitious at a 2% delivery-GB penetration ceiling, with enterprise catch-up and new grocery/retail/mobility ad formats as the near-term unlock (§11 Opportunities, §12 Play #4). Convert the Mobility→Eats gap into revenue with an AI-personalized nudge aimed at the 30% of Mobility riders who've never ordered Eats, using demand that's already free and app-to-app (§11, §12 Play #3). In Europe, stop playing defense against DoorDash and Prosus and replicate the Finland playbook — organic, membership-led growth that took the market to #1 on launch — instead of competing on intensity alone (§11). And tighten AI ROI tracking product by product so the AI spend overrun reads as disciplined investment rather than an admitted budgeting miss (§11).

**5. How would you compete with it?**
Not on liquidity at global scale — that moat is real and compounding (§5 Moats). Two real angles. First, attack where the AV thesis is structurally dependent on other companies' choices: Uber's whole AV strategy assumes AV makers choose to list on its 3P layer rather than go direct, but a well-capitalized AV maker with owned supply and demand — Tesla is the one named in the dossier — could skip the 3P tax entirely in dense downtown metros, exactly the markets with the highest visibility and where novelty already pulls new riders fastest (§5 Weaknesses, §9 Contrarian bets). Price below Uber's take-rate-inclusive fare since there's no marketplace cut to share, and concentrate on the 30% of bookings that come from major cities rather than the suburban 70% where Uber's profit and membership lock-in are deepest (§2, §5) — attack the thin edge, not the moat. Second, attack the planned-travel segment the dossier itself flags as underbuilt: "planned surfaces beyond airports are underbuilt" (§7 Segment B gap) — a corporate-travel or events-logistics specialist could out-execute Reserve on guaranteed, pre-booked certainty without needing Uber's spontaneous-ride liquidity at all.

**6. What would you 10x?**
The AV utilization marketplace (§12 Play #6, sized 100×) — package "list your AV on Uber for +30% utilization plus trough backfill" as the default go-to-market for every AV maker, not just the >30 partners already signed. The mechanism already works (AVs on Uber run 30% more trips per vehicle per day than standalone, §3 Wow Vault) and the network already owns the only demand pool — rides, delivery, freight — that can fill the Saturday-to-Monday trough (§3, §5 Moats). The 10× move is turning that into the industry-standard route to market so no AV maker seriously considers going direct.

**7. Design the GM's 1-page daily dashboard.**
Anchored on the actual North star (§8 Strategy: gross bookings per active member per period, capturing liquidity × frequency × cross-sell × retention in one number). The dashboard tracks: MAPC growth split across Mobility/Delivery/Freight/Advertising (§1, §2); Uber One penetration (share of bookings, now >50%) and multi-product attach (40% use more than one product), since that's the retention engine (§2); the revenue-to-gross-bookings spread, the live signal on take-rate/mix health (§2, §5); AV trips per vehicle per day versus 1P and AV trip growth (already >10× Y/Y), the leading indicator on the AV thesis (§3); and a price-consistency index tracking whether insurance savings keep flowing back to riders as flat or falling prices (§8's own leading metric for the "Now" roadmap item).

**8. Pick the 1-2 metrics the whole company should focus on.**
Gross bookings per active member per period — the actual North star (§8 Strategy), since it compounds liquidity, frequency, cross-sell, and retention into one number rather than any single input metric. And the revenue-to-gross-bookings growth gap — not an official headline metric, but the one number that recurs across the weaknesses table, the contrarian bets, and the mistakes section as the tell on whether take-rate or mix is eroding (§5, §9, §11). Track those two and you catch both the health of the flywheel and the first sign of margin trouble.

**9. Build a me-too version in a few months — what's essential?**
Strip §1 (Business anatomy) to the mechanism: a two-sided marketplace where more riders shorten ETAs and lower prices, which pulls in more drivers, which shortens ETAs further — that loop, plus a cheap entry product to prime first-time acquisition (Wait & Save-style, since low-cost products already show a +75% frequency lift) and a straightforward take-rate on completed trips. Freight, Advertising, Uber One, Reserve, the barbell premium tier, and the entire AV-partnership layer are all real value creators today (§1, §3, §12) but none are load-bearing for a working clone — they're what gets added once the core liquidity loop is already turning.

**10. Pick one area, be the PM — walk through how you'd design it.**
Segment B, the certainty-maximizing planner (§7) — the dossier's own gap flag: "planned surfaces beyond airports are underbuilt." Job: guarantee I make the flight or the meeting, no anxiety. Friction: on-demand rideshare can't promise a car is there at 5am, and the planned-experience surface today stops at airports and hotels. Aha: "Reserve had a car waiting the second I landed" (§7B), already true for airports — the design extends that same guarantee across a planned day: pre-booked pickup ahead of any calendar event, a guaranteed-wait-time contract backed by higher driver pay (the mechanism that already makes Reserve work), and hotel-style credits folded into Uber One so the planner never feels like they're paying a premium for certainty.

**11. Cut the scope to 1/4 — what survives?**
The core liquidity loop serving Segment A, the cost-minimizing spontaneous rider (§7A) — still the largest job, the one the whole ETA/price flywheel depends on — plus Segment E, the sparse-market/suburban user (§7E), since that's where roughly 75% of US profit already sits and where the strategy explicitly says to prioritize over dense-downtown vanity metrics (§8 Strategy). Uber One survives as the retention layer since it already carries >50% of bookings (§2). Cut: Reserve/premium (Segment C, a profit-growth story rather than a segment the business needs to survive), the multi-service household push (Segment D, the deepest cross-sell dependency and so the most peripheral to a bare mobility business), and the entire AV-partnership program, which is a bet on the next decade rather than what keeps the current business alive.

**12. Pitch an engineer who wants hard problems.**
The real-time, cross-marketplace demand-pooling problem behind AV utilization (§3 Wow Vault, §5 Moats): matching a single AV against three structurally different demand types — a person needing a ride, a grocery order, a freight load — to keep utilization high through a demand swing that drops about 45% from the Saturday peak to the Monday trough, at national scale, with sub-few-minute ETA guarantees on the passenger side. That's a live, continuously-running optimization problem nobody else runs at this scale, because nobody else owns three different marketplaces sharing the same physical asset.

**13. Rebuild/remarket for a totally different, untargeted audience.**
Not individual riders paying per trip — healthcare payers and insurers who need to move members who can't drive themselves: non-emergency medical transport, elderly and disability mobility contracts. That's the same suburban/sparse-market liquidity advantage (§7 Segment E gap: "still under-penetrated… the profit engine with the most headroom," ~75% of US profit, 1.5–2× faster growth) pointed at a completely different payer — not a consumer swiping a card per ride, but a healthcare system or insurer paying per contracted member per month. Uber already has the dense supply and ETA reliability in exactly the geographies — suburban, rural — where this need is worst-served today; it's a rebrand of an asset already built, for a buyer that was never on the roadmap.

**14. Remove the product constraint entirely — what else would you build?**
Drop "must be a vehicle a person rides in." Push Play #6 (§12, AV utilization marketplace) to its limit: Uber becomes the demand-routing utility for any autonomous physical asset that needs to stay busy — delivery robots, drones, last-mile warehouse robots — sold the same pitch already proven on cars: "list on Uber for higher utilization and trough backfill." This still passes the right-to-win test (§12): the asset that makes it work isn't hardware, it's what Uber already owns — three simultaneous demand pools (rides, delivery, freight) that can backfill any autonomous asset's idle hours, plus the AV data-sharing relationship with NVIDIA that already positions Uber as neutral infrastructure the whole autonomy industry, not just carmakers, could route through.
