The Framework

How a dossier gets built

Each company page on this site is one living file, not a snapshot. It grows every time a new earnings call, article, or press release gets fed into it: merged into the existing page, not appended as a new block. A dossier from six months ago and a dossier updated this morning look identical in structure; only the content sharpens.

The arc is always the same: Facts → Insights → Differentiators → Plays. Start with what's true and dated. Build up to what's non-obvious. Use that to name what's actually defensible. End with concrete moves a management team could run next, tied to something the company specifically owns.

The Wow Filter

Most facts about a big company are things a sharp interviewer or investor already knows. The first section of every dossier, the Wow Vault, exists to filter for the other kind: the mechanism behind a metric, a contrarian bet with real reasoning behind it, a reframe worth borrowing, a tension the company itself hasn't resolved. Generic facts get compressed to one line or pushed into the reference tables further down the page. The bar for the top section is narrow on purpose: would a sharp reader not already know this, or be impressed by the framing?

Positioning before segmentation

Segmentation answers who a company serves. Positioning answers where it's playing, and it has to come first. Segmenting a market before you know its shape just reproduces someone else's map. Every dossier's strategy section starts by finding two creative axes (never the reflexive price-vs-quality default) and plotting where the company sits relative to competitors on them. The interesting finding is usually the empty quadrant: the space a company is claiming that its two nearest competitors structurally can't follow into without breaking their own business model.

Moats, scored against seven patterns

"They have a moat" is the least useful sentence in a strategy memo. Every moat claim in these dossiers gets scored against Hamilton Helmer's seven power types: scale economies, network economies, counter-positioning, switching costs, branding, cornered resources, process power. Most companies hold two or three of the seven, genuinely. Naming which ones they don't hold is as informative as the ones they do. It shows exactly where a well-funded competitor would compete on even terms.

Needs-based segments, never personas

Segments are cut on what a person is trying to get done, not on age, income, or a name like "Busy Sarah." A needs-based segment has to pass five tests before it earns a place in the dossier: the people in it share a consistent need, that need is specific to this product category, the segment is reachable, it can be prioritized against the others, and the company can plausibly win and defend it. Demographic groupings and composite personas fail this test by construction. They describe who someone is, not what they need.

Eigenquestions

Most companies have one or two questions that, once answered, resolve half the open debates about them at once: an eigenquestion, the way an eigenvector captures the dominant axis of a much larger space. Finding it means reframing the situation from a few different angles, generating far more candidate questions than you need, and keeping only the ones with real discriminating power. Every dossier ends with its current best answer to "what's the one thing that would change this read," plus a specific, checkable signal for when that answer flips.

Sourcing and corrections

Every non-obvious claim carries a source tag, traceable to an earnings call, a filing, an investor day, or a dated article. Estimates are labeled as estimates. When a new source corrects an old number, the correction is shown in the open rather than silently overwritten. An early estimate that turns out wrong is part of the record, not an embarrassment to hide.
